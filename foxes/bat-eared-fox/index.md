This fox carries an impressive pair of listening dishes. The **bat-eared
fox**, **Otocyon megalotis**, uses its ears to locate small prey, especially
insects. Despite its name, it is not part bat: it is a member of **Canidae**,
the dog family, in the genus **Otocyon** rather than **Vulpes**.

It shares large ears with the [fennec fox]({{ '/foxes/fennec-fox/' | relative_url }}),
but those two species are not interchangeable. This is a hunter of African
short-grass country, with unusual teeth and a family life worth a close look.

## How do you recognize a bat-eared fox?

Look for enormous ears above a small face, sandy-gray fur, dark lower legs,
and dark markings around the eyes and muzzle. The backs of the ears and the
tip of its bushy tail are dark too. San Diego Zoo describes pale or buff fur
inside the ears and across the forehead.

Its ears can exceed **13 cm** in height in San Diego Zoo's description. The
Smithsonian gives a head-and-body length of **46-66 cm**, plus a tail of
about **30 cm**. Do not add the ear length to those figures: ears, body,
and tail are separate measurements.

## Where do bat-eared foxes live?

San Diego Zoo describes populations in **eastern and southern Africa**, in
short-grass savannas and scrublands. A **savanna** is grassland with scattered
trees or shrubs. A map of Africa is not a map of continuous bat-eared fox
habitat: suitable places and important food sources are unevenly distributed.

Short vegetation makes it easier to move and forage near the ground. Their
range is closely associated with harvester termites, an important food.
That connection is an example of how a predator's distribution can depend
on its prey rather than on temperature alone.

## What do those ears listen for?

A hunting fox moves slowly with its nose near the ground and its ears
positioned to listen. When it locates insects, it can dig or make a quick
pounce. Sound helps reveal a meal that may be concealed beneath the surface.
Those ears also contain blood vessels and help release heat.

Do not imagine it uses a bat's echolocation. The name describes the large
ears, not a bat's ancestry or hunting equipment. Listening for prey is
also different from making a sound and using its echo to find objects.

## Why does this fox have extra teeth?

An adult has **46-50 teeth**, according to San Diego Zoo, compared with the
[red fox's usual 42]({{ '/foxes/red-fox/#teeth-and-tracks-the-close-up-clues' | relative_url }}).
Its extra **molars**, the teeth toward the back of the mouth, help process
insect food. San Diego Zoo describes three upper and four lower molars on
each side, rather than the two upper and three lower common in other canids.

The useful fact is the extra crushing equipment, not a sweeping claim that
it has more teeth than every other mammal. Tooth counts vary across mammals,
and comparisons should name the group being compared. Even within this
species, the reported adult range is not a single fixed number.

## What is on the menu?

**Insectivorous** means feeding mainly on insects. The Smithsonian estimates
that insects account for roughly **90-95%** of the bat-eared fox's diet,
including harvester termites, beetles, and grubs. San Diego Zoo reports
termites making up **up to 70%**. These figures do not mean the same thing:
one is all insects, while the other is termites alone.

Neither figure is a guaranteed menu for every fox in every season. Other
foods can include small vertebrates, eggs, and some plant material. Beetles
associated with animal droppings can be another opportunity: a fox near a
herd may be looking for insects, not hunting the large hoofed animals.

## When are these foxes active?

There is more to the timetable than "all foxes come out at night." San Diego
Zoo describes bat-eared foxes in eastern Africa as mostly active at night;
in southern Africa they can be nocturnal in hot summers and more active by
day during winter. Temperature and the activity of their insect prey help
shape the schedule.

**Diurnal** means active during the day; **nocturnal** means active at night.
They describe patterns, not rules forbidding an animal from moving at any
other time. Family members also communicate through ear and tail positions,
scents, and sounds including soft calls.

## What is inside a bat-eared fox den?

Families use underground burrows for shelter and to raise young. They can
dig their own or enlarge an unused burrow made by another animal. San Diego
Zoo describes several den holes within a territory, with multiple entrances,
tunnels, and chambers. Old termite mounds can be used too, but that does not
mean every family lives inside an active termite colony.

## What makes a fox father so important?

The Smithsonian describes males doing much of the early kit care: guarding,
grooming, and sometimes carrying young to another den. That allows the
mother to spend time foraging to produce milk. **Parental care** includes
protecting and teaching young, not just nursing them.

The Smithsonian reports **60-70 days of gestation**, **1-6 pups**, and nursing
for about **14-15 weeks**. This profile follows that account consistently for
those figures. Both parents contribute, and growing kits learn foraging
skills before becoming independent. It is a team effort, not a case of one
parent doing nothing.

### Separate a food group from one food
{: #bat-eared-notebook-heading}

Write "insects" as a large circle, with "termites" inside it. Add beetles and
grasshoppers elsewhere inside the big circle. Why can a percentage for all
insects differ from a percentage for termites? Record the source beside each
number rather than treating two different measurements as a contradiction.
</aside>

## How can we be good wildlife neighbors?

Watch from a distance, never feed or handle a fox, and leave den entrances
alone. Stay on permitted routes and avoid driving across burrow sites.
These are wild animals, not pets, even when photographs make their ears
look irresistibly soft.

The Smithsonian identifies habitat loss and human capture among threats.
Learning about their role as insect predators is more helpful than calling
them pests because they happen to visit farmland. If a wild animal needs
help, ask an adult to contact trained local wildlife professionals.
