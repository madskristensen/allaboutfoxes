Meet a fox built for open tundra, not Antarctic ice. The Arctic fox lives
around the northern polar region, including Alaska, northern Canada,
Greenland, Iceland, and northern Eurasia. **Circumpolar** means distributed
around a pole; it does not mean every patch of ice is suitable habitat.

Its scientific name, **Vulpes lagopus**, includes a clue: *lagopus* means
"rabbit-footed," referring to its furry feet. This is a true member of the
dog family, not a rabbit relative. Compared with a red fox, it has a shorter
muzzle, smaller rounded ears, shorter legs, and a compact silhouette.

## How do you recognize an Arctic fox?

Look at proportions before color. A winter Arctic fox is rounded and densely
furred; a summer animal can look surprisingly slim after molting. It does
not have the red fox's typical black stockings or a sharply contrasting
white tail tip. Ear shape and the short muzzle remain useful clues after
the winter fluff disappears.

### White morph or blue morph?

A **morph** is a color form within a species. White-morph Arctic foxes grow
white winter fur and brown-gray summer fur with paler undersides. Blue-morph
foxes remain dark or charcoal-colored year-round, becoming somewhat lighter
in winter, rather than turning white. "Blue" does not mean bright sky-blue!
Both morphs can occur in one litter.

Alaska Fish and Game describes white-morph foxes shedding their winter coat
from early April, with short brown summer fur by late June. The winter coat
grows during September and October and is complete by November. Those are
Alaskan seasonal observations, not a calendar guaranteed everywhere in the
Arctic. Day length, latitude, and local conditions matter when comparing
photos. The blue morph is more common on Alaska's Aleutian and Pribilof
Islands than in northern populations.

## How does an Arctic fox keep warm?

Dense fur traps air and slows the escape of body heat. The winter coat even
covers the soles of the feet. Short ears, short legs, and a compact body
reduce exposed surface area relative to body volume. These are **adaptations**:
inherited features that help an animal survive and reproduce in its habitat.

The thick tail can cover the muzzle when a fox curls up to rest. The National
Park Service also describes specialized blood circulation in the paws that
helps protect against cold. No single feature is a magic heater; fur, body
shape, circulation, food energy, and sheltered resting places work together.
Arctic foxes **do not hibernate**. They still need to find food through winter.

## Where do tundra foxes travel?

Arctic and alpine **tundra** has low-growing plants rather than a forest
canopy. Foxes use coastal plains, rocky shores, and inland tundra. Sea ice
can become a winter travel route and feeding ground, where they scavenge
seal remains left by polar bears. They are terrestrial mammals using the
ice surface, not animals that live underwater.

A fox's breeding area and its winter journeys need not be the same size.
Alaska Fish and Game reports movements of hundreds of kilometers and a fox
tagged on the Russian coast that was captured near Wainwright, Alaska, a
year later. That is evidence of long-distance movement, not proof that all
Arctic foxes make the same annual migration.

## What is on the Arctic menu?

There is no single Arctic fox menu. Inland foxes often depend on **lemmings**
and tundra voles; foxes near seabird cliffs may rely heavily on auklets,
puffins, or murres. Eggs, carrion, and sometimes berries add other food
sources. **Carrion** means an animal that is already dead, so scavenging is
not the same as making a fresh kill.

Where lemmings and voles are important prey, fox numbers rise and fall with
prey cycles. Food abundance influences how many pups can be raised; a large
litter is not a promise of many surviving adults. Coastal food webs can
work differently from inland ones, so a lemming-based explanation should
not be stretched to every island population.

Surplus food can be stored in a **cache**, such as among rocks or in a den,
and retrieved later. A stored meal lets a fox use a short-lived abundance
of food after that opportunity has passed. Never add human food to a fox's
supplies: feeding changes wild behavior and can create dangerous encounters.

## What is life like in an Arctic fox den?

Breeding foxes excavate sandy, well-drained soil on mounds or riverbanks;
they may enlarge ground squirrel burrows. Multiple entrances connect to
underground passages. Good drainage matters when the surface soil thaws:
a nursery needs shelter, not standing water. Some long-used den sites are
reoccupied by generations of foxes.

### Peek beneath the tundra

This cutaway has four entrances in a low tundra mound, branching tunnels,
and a sheltered nursery. Animal Diversity Web describes dens with **4-8
entrances** and tunnel systems covering about **30 square meters**, but real
sites can be simpler or more extensive. Our four openings are an example,
not a rule. The frozen-ground boundary is schematic; its depth and the
seasonally thawed layer vary by site and time of year.

### From newborn to young hunter

Alaska Fish and Game reports about **52 days of gestation**, litters averaging
**seven pups**, and litters of up to **15**. These figures describe that
account, not a biological maximum across all populations. Newborn pups of
both morphs have short, dark-brown fur; a white adult does not give birth to
miniature snow-white pups.

Pups first emerge at about **three weeks**, begin eating meat at about **one
month**, and are fully weaned at roughly **six weeks** in the Alaska account.
Both parents bring food. At around **three months**, young foxes begin hunting
and ranging farther from the den; family units gradually break up during
September and October. Development varies, and high first-year mortality
makes survival very different from simply reaching weaning age.

## How can we keep Arctic foxes wild?

Watch from a distance with an adult, use binoculars, and obey local wildlife
rules. Never visit a den for a closer photo, offer food, or try to touch a
fox. If it changes behavior because of you, give it more room. Secure food
and rubbish around camps, and keep pets under control.

Alaska's species account lists the Arctic fox as globally **Least Concern**,
but a global category is not a guarantee that every local population is
secure. Food fluctuations, disease, and competition can matter locally.
Check regional conservation sources rather than treating the entire Arctic
as one identical habitat. If a fox seems injured or unusually approachable,
keep away and ask an adult to contact wildlife professionals.
