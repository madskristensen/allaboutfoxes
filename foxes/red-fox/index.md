If you could design an animal for a secret woodland adventure, it might look
a lot like a red fox. Pointed ears help it listen, a narrow muzzle helps it
investigate, and a magnificent tail follows along behind.

But this fox is not just a woodland animal. It can live near farms, in
grasslands, in mountains, and even in towns. Its secret is being
**adaptable**: able to make use of different places and different foods.

## How do you recognize a red fox?

Look for a slender face, tall triangular ears, dark lower legs, and a long,
bushy tail that often has a white tip. Many red foxes wear rusty orange-red
fur with a pale chest, as if they have pulled on a fluffy scarf.

Those markings are clues, not a uniform! Some red foxes have much darker
coats. A **silver fox** is a color form of the red fox, not a separate species.
Another color form, called a cross fox, has dark markings across its back
and shoulders.

## Where do red foxes live?

Red foxes live across much of the Northern Hemisphere, including large parts
of North America, Europe, Asia, and northern Africa. People have also
introduced them to places such as Australia, where they can harm native
wildlife. Being a successful explorer is not always good news for the animals
already living somewhere.

A red fox often likes a mixture of cover and open ground: somewhere to hide,
somewhere to rest, and somewhere to hunt. That could be the edge of a forest
or a patchwork of fields and hedges. Some find suitable homes in cities, too.

**Explorer word: habitat.** An animal's habitat is the place where it finds
the food, water, shelter, and space it needs.

## What is on the menu?

Red foxes are **omnivores**, which means they eat both animals and plant food.
Their meals can include mice, voles, rabbits, insects, birds, and fruit.
What they eat depends on where they live and what is available.

Watch a hunting fox from a safe distance and you might see a special move:
it stops, listens, and springs into the air. Its front feet come down toward
the little animal it has detected. This pounce can help it catch prey hidden
by grass or snow.

A fox may also hide extra food to eat later. Scientists call a hidden food
store a **cache**. Think of it as a fox's tiny outdoor pantry.

## What is that tail for?

That beautiful tail is useful equipment. It helps with balance as a fox moves
and turns. When the fox curls up to rest, its tail can wrap around its body
to help keep it warm.

The tail is also part of a fox's body language. Foxes communicate in more
ways than one: with postures, scents, and sounds such as barks. A quiet
woodland has a lot of messages we cannot easily notice!

## What is life like for a fox family?

Young foxes are called **kits**, cubs, or pups. They are born with their eyes
closed and depend on adults for food and care. A sheltered den gives the
family a place to raise its young.

Adults may dig a den or use a burrow made by another animal. As the kits grow,
they begin venturing outside, playing, and learning about their surroundings.
Both parents can help provide food, and sometimes older offspring help too.

Red foxes often hunt alone rather than in a big pack. They are frequently
active around dusk and at night, but a fox out in daylight is not unusual,
especially when adults are busy finding food for growing kits.

### Try a fox detective notebook

Draw a fox and label three features you could use to recognize it. Then
write one question you still have. Would you like to know how its ears work,
how a kit learns to hunt, or how different its winter coat feels?

You can be a wildlife detective with a book or a photo, too. You do not need
to find a wild fox to make a great discovery.
</aside>

## How can we be good fox neighbors?

The best way to enjoy a wild fox is to let it stay wild. Watch quietly from
a distance with an adult. Never approach a den, try to touch a fox, or offer
it food. Feeding wild animals can teach them to approach people, which can
put both animals and people in danger.

Keep pets under control, put litter in secure bins, and follow local wildlife
rules. If you think a fox is injured or needs help, ask an adult to contact
a qualified local wildlife organization rather than trying to handle it.
